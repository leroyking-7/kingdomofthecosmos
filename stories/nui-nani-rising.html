Prologue - Tidal cycle III

The sky above Nivel Station had turned the color of iron. Low clouds churned like machinery gone mad, pulsing with pressure that made the ground feel hollow. Deep below, seismic alarms screamed — not in bursts, but continuously, like the world itself was out of breath.

Scientist Marek Valen stood alone in the upper observatory, surrounded by flickering consoles and blinking diagnostics. Most of the staff had already evacuated, forced down the slopes by panicked command protocols. Marek remained.

Something was wrong. Terribly wrong.

The tidal shift had arrived early, way too early. Every model, every projection, had placed the next Reset at least nine years away. They were wrong. Or someone had falsified the data. The core destabilization was already happening.

He wasn’t trying to survive.

He was trying to salvage truth.

The digital recorders were already glitching. Electromagnetic pulses were frying systems in waves. The deeper feeds were corrupted beyond recovery. Only one thing still functioned — his personal field journal, shielded in a magnetic wrap. Analog. Fragile. But immune to the storm.

Marek flipped to a blank page and began writing furiously. Names. Locations. Date strings. Schematic fragments. And in the corner of one page, he scribbled an unverified coordinate set — found in the drift archives last year. The origin was unknown. Possibly non-Veridorian.

He circled it twice.

Another tremor struck. This one deeper — more violent. The mountain cracked open somewhere to the west. Steam howled upward like a wounded animal. The ground buckled. A warning light exploded above the hatch.

He staggered to the emergency drift chute — a narrow tunnel used for geological core samples. He sealed the journal in a containment shell and shoved it down the shaft.

He whispered something — a word lost to the roar of the cracking earth.

Then the floor split.

Marek Valen didn’t make it to the lower levels.

Chapter I

I stood by the window on the twenty-first floor as the sky surrendered to night. Veridora never fell into darkness all at once—it faded, layer by layer, until even the air felt still.

Far above, Nui Nani breached the horizon. Full and luminant, it cast its pale shimmer across the water. Somewhere behind the planet’s shadow, Ula Iki waited. Between the two moons, there was always a sliver of time when neither ruled the sky—a breath between light and dark.
